## Two different licences {#two-different-licences}

This distinction decides your whole first year in Germany:

- **Approbation** — the full, permanent, nationwide licence. Unrestricted practice.
- **Berufserlaubnis** (§10 BÄO) — a temporary, restricted permission, usually limited to
  two years, one Bundesland, and one named employer, granted while you work toward
  Approbation. Many international doctors start here.

## Qualification route (in Germany) {#qualification-route-in-germany}

- **Medical school:** ~6 years and 3 months, ending in the **Dritter Abschnitt der
  Ärztlichen Prüfung**. Admission is brutally competitive and largely NC-driven.
- **Approbation** is granted on passing.
- **Facharztweiterbildung** (specialist training): a further **5–6 years** depending on
  specialty, supervised, regulated by the Landesärztekammer, ending in the
  Facharztprüfung. You are employed and paid as an **Assistenzarzt** throughout.

A long-running reform of the **Approbationsordnung für Ärzte (ÄApprO)** has been in
preparation for years and keeps slipping. Check its current status rather than trusting any
written summary, including this one.

## Pay {#pay}

Hospital doctors are covered by dedicated collective agreements — **TV-Ärzte/VKA**
(municipal hospitals) or **TV-Ärzte/TdL** (university hospitals). Figures below are
**TV-Ärzte/VKA**, from the table valid **01.06.2026 – 31.12.2026**, verified 2026-09,
excluding on-call. The agreement grades by **Stufe**, not by calendar year, and TdL
differs:

| Stage | Gross/month |
|---|---|
| Assistenzarzt (Stufe I, 1→6) | €5,722 – €7,355 |
| Facharzt (Stufe II) | €7,552 – €9,699 |
| Oberarzt (Stufe III) | €9,460 – €10,811 |
| Leitender Oberarzt (Stufe IV) | €11,128 – €11,923 |
| Chefarzt | Individually negotiated, outside the agreement |

**Bereitschaftsdienst and Rufbereitschaft** (on-call) add substantially — for many
Assistenzärzte this is 20–30% on top. Note that on-call pay is a perennial source of
disputes; read the Dienstplan terms before signing.

Established practice owners (niedergelassene Ärzte) earn on a completely different model
via the Kassenärztliche Vereinigung, and require a **Kassensitz** — a limited, tradeable
licence to treat statutory-insurance patients.

## Foreign-trained candidates {#foreign-trained-candidates}

1. **EU/EEA/Switzerland:** medical degrees are automatically recognised under Directive
   2005/36/EC. You apply for Approbation and, subject to language, get it.
2. **Third countries:** the Landesprüfungsamt runs a **Gleichwertigkeitsprüfung**. If your
   training is found not equivalent, you sit a **Kenntnisprüfung** — an oral-practical exam
   at the level of the German final medical exam, covering internal medicine and surgery
   plus a third area. It is demanding; failure rates are high, and you generally get three
   attempts.
3. **Fachsprachprüfung (FSP):** a medical-German exam at the Landesärztekammer —
   patient interview, documentation, doctor-to-doctor handover. Required in essentially
   every state. The formal entry level is **B2 general + C1 medical**; in practice, plan
   for C1.
4. **Visa:** §16d AufenthG while pursuing recognition, or the skilled-worker/EU Blue Card
   route once licensed. Doctors qualify for the Blue Card comfortably on salary.

## Pitfalls {#pitfalls}

- **The FSP is the real bottleneck**, not clinical knowledge. Doctors with excellent
  everyday German routinely fail it on documentation and register-switching.
- **State shopping is common and imperfectly legal-grey** — processing times and
  Kenntnisprüfung waiting lists differ hugely by Bundesland. Changing state mid-process
  can restart it.
- **Berufserlaubnis is a trap if you get comfortable.** It is time-limited; some doctors
  drift for years in restricted roles without converting to Approbation.
- **Your specialty may not map.** Facharzt recognition is a separate procedure from
  Approbation, decided by the Landesärztekammer, and years of foreign specialist training
  are often only partly credited.
