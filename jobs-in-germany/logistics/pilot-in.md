> The extreme case of a theme that runs through this repo: **what it costs to be allowed to do the work.** Germany's other qualifications are paid (Ausbildung), fee-free (university) or a few hundred euro ([occupational certificates](/jobs-in-germany/reference/occupational-certificates/)). An airline licence costs a six-figure sum, and you generally pay it before you have a job.

## The licence stack {#the-licence-stack}

European, not German. A German ATPL is an **EASA** licence and is valid across the EASA states — genuinely portable, like the [train driver's](/jobs-in-germany/logistics/triebfahrzeugfuehrer-in/), and unlike almost everything else here.

| Element | What it is |
|---|---|
| **PPL(A)** | Private licence. The first step on the modular route |
| **CPL(A)** | Commercial licence — you may be paid to fly |
| **ATPL(A)** | The airline licence. Theory is passed early; the full licence requires hours |
| **MPL** | An airline-specific alternative, tied to one operator from the start |
| **Type Rating** | Per aircraft type. **Without it you cannot fly that aircraft** |
| **Medical Class 1** | Annual, and more often with age. **This is the career's single point of failure** |

**Everything above expires.** Proficiency checks roughly every six months, line checks, recurrent training, and the Class 1 medical every year. A cardiac, neurological, visual or metabolic finding can end the career outright, which is why **loss-of-licence insurance** is standard in the profession and should be treated as essential rather than optional.

## The routes in, and what they cost {#the-routes-in-and-what-they-cost}

| Route | Shape | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| **Integrated ATPL** at an ATO | 18–24 months full time, zero to frozen ATPL | **€80,000 – €120,000+**, self-funded |
| **Modular** | PPL → hour building → CPL/IR/ATPL theory, in stages | Similar total, spread out, often alongside work |
| **Airline cadet programme** | Selection by the carrier, training at its academy | Still largely **self-funded or loan-funded** — the fully sponsored model ended years ago |
| **Bundeswehr** | Military flying, then a transition to civil aviation | No fee; a long service commitment instead |

**The debt is the defining fact.** Trainees commonly finish with a six-figure loan, no guaranteed job, and a **type rating** that may also have to be paid for before a first contract. Hour-building as a flight instructor is the traditional bridge and pays poorly.

The repo's convention is honesty about sectors, so: this is a career whose financial risk is front-loaded onto the individual to a degree matched by nothing else in this collection. It is also cyclical — hiring collapses in downturns and returns sharply, and the people caught by a downturn are the ones who have just borrowed the money.

## Pay {#pay}

**Market estimate.** Cockpit pay is set by carrier-level agreements — **Vereinigung Cockpit** negotiates at the legacy carriers — and varies enormously between a network airline, a low-cost operator and a contract arrangement.

| Stage | Gross/month |
|---|---|
| Flight instructor, hour building | €1,800 – €2,800 |
| First Officer, low-cost or regional, entry | €3,500 – €5,500 |
| First Officer, network carrier, entry | €5,500 – €7,000 |
| First Officer, experienced | €7,000 – €9,500 |
| **Captain, network carrier** | **€10,000 – €16,000+** |
| Cargo, business aviation | Wide range, often comparable to network |

Add to this: **per-diems (Spesen)**, which are substantially tax-free — see [tax and net pay](/jobs-in-germany/reference/taxes-and-net-pay/) — and, at legacy carriers, pension arrangements that are a significant part of total compensation.

Subtract from it: the loan. A first officer's early years frequently net out closer to a skilled trade once repayment is counted.

**Watch the employment model.** Some operators have used contractor, agency or nominally self-employed arrangements for cockpit crew. That shifts social insurance, sick pay and pension onto you and is worth far less than the same headline figure at a directly employed carrier. Check what the contract actually is — see [employment basics](/jobs-in-germany/reference/employment-basics/).

## Working conditions {#working-conditions}

- **Flight Time Limitations** under EU air-operations rules cap duty and flight hours and mandate rest. They are a safety floor, not a comfortable schedule
- **Standby, early starts, night flights, time-zone disruption** — fatigue is the profession's chronic occupational issue and is formally managed
- **Base assignment** is the employer's, and it can move. Living where you are based is not guaranteed
- **Seniority** governs almost everything — upgrade to Captain, roster preference, base, aircraft type. Changing airline usually means starting at the bottom of a new list, which is why pilots move employer rarely

## Demand and outlook {#demand-and-outlook}

**Cyclical, currently favourable, and structurally supported by retirements.** Large cohorts reach the mandatory age limits this decade, and traffic has recovered.

Honest qualifications:

- The cycle is severe. Downturns stop hiring completely, and there is no partial version of that for someone holding fresh debt.
- **Single-pilot operations** for commercial flights are under discussion at regulator level and are a genuine long-horizon question for anyone starting now — not imminent, not fanciful.
- European carriers compete on cost, and cockpit terms at new entrants are materially worse than the legacy standard this profession's reputation is built on.

## Foreign-trained candidates {#foreign-trained-candidates}

1. **The licence is not a German vocational qualification**, which makes the usual routes in this repo fit badly. §18a expects a recognised two-year-plus vocational qualification; an ATPL is not one.
2. **EASA licence holders** — from any EASA state — can be hired directly. This is the normal case, and it makes **EU/EEA right to work the decisive credential**, more than the licence itself.
3. **Third-country (ICAO) licences convert**, but not cheaply: EASA theory examinations, a skill test, and often a type rating. Budget time and money and start with the LBA.
4. **Language: ICAO English Level 4 is the legal minimum** for the licence; Level 6 is native-equivalent. **German is not legally required to fly** — but German carriers commonly require it for the job, and it is the main non-technical filter on hiring here. See [language certificates](/jobs-in-germany/reference/language-certificates/).
5. **Visa:** there is no clean skilled-worker route; in practice non-EU pilots are hired by a carrier that arranges the permit, and that is uncommon while EASA-licensed EU candidates are available.
6. **Security vetting** under aviation-security law applies to everyone.

## Pitfalls {#pitfalls}

- **Do not begin training without a Class 1 medical in hand.** It is the cheapest test of whether the career is possible at all, and it is routinely left too late.
- **The type rating may be yours to pay for.** Ask, before accepting an offer.
- **"Self-employed first officer" is a warning sign**, not a tax optimisation.
- **Choose the ATO carefully.** Training quality, examiner relationships and airline pipelines vary, and the money is not recoverable.
- **Plan for the loss of the medical.** Insurance, and a second qualification, are what responsible people in this profession have.
