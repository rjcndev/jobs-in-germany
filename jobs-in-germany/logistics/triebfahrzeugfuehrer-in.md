## The licence has two parts, and only one of them is yours {#the-licence-has-two-parts-and-only-one-of-them-is-yours}

Under the **Triebfahrzeugführerscheinverordnung (TfV)**, implementing EU Directive
2007/59/EC:

| | **Triebfahrzeugführerschein** | **Zusatzbescheinigung** |
|---|---|---|
| Issued by | **Eisenbahn-Bundesamt** | **Your employer** — the railway undertaking |
| Covers | That you are fit and competent to drive, generally | **Which vehicles** and **which routes** you may actually work |
| Valid | EU-wide, **10 years**, subject to medical checks | Only with that employer |
| On changing jobs | **Goes with you** | **Lost.** The new employer trains and issues its own |

This is the single most important practical fact about the profession. **Baureihenkenntnis**
(vehicle type knowledge) and **Streckenkunde** (route knowledge) are employer-specific and
must be rebuilt on every move — which is why train drivers change employer less often than
the labour shortage would suggest, and why a new employer's offer includes weeks of
route learning before you are productive.

**Fitness is continuing, not a one-off.** Medical and psychological aptitude examinations are
required to obtain the licence and are repeated periodically, with the interval shortening
with age. Failing one ends the career, which is a real risk to plan around — more so than in
almost any other occupation here.

## Two ways in {#two-ways-in}

### 1. The Ausbildung {#1-the-ausbildung}

**Eisenbahner/in im Betriebsdienst, Fachrichtung Lokführer und Transport** — a three-year
dual Ausbildung, paid, examined by the IHK. It covers more than driving: shunting, train
preparation, operational rules, and the signalling and dispatch side. It is the broader
qualification and the better base for a career that may later move into dispatching,
instruction or operations management.

### 2. Quereinstieg — the reason this file exists {#2-quereinstieg--the-reason-this-file-exists}

A **retraining of roughly nine to twelve months**, ending in the licence and the employer's
certificate. Open to career changers from any background, typically with:

- minimum age **20** for the unrestricted licence
- a completed vocational qualification or equivalent work history
- medical and psychological fitness, tested before you are accepted
- shift-work suitability, meaning genuinely so

**It is generally paid for by the employer**, with a training salary during it and a tie-in
clause afterwards — read that clause, it is real and it is enforceable. Where an employer is
not funding it, the **[Bildungsgutschein](/jobs-in-germany/reference/weiterbildung-funding/)** does:
train driving sits on every shortage list and is one of the most commonly funded retrainings
in Germany.

Nothing else in this repo converts an unrelated career into a licensed, shortage-occupation,
tariff-paid job this quickly. That is the honest attraction, and it is why the sector
recruits so heavily on it.

## What the job involves {#what-the-job-involves}

Driving, and a great deal that is not driving: train preparation and brake tests, operating
rules (**Ril 408**), signal observation, radio with the dispatcher (**Fahrdienstleiter**),
shunting, fault handling, and — in freight — long periods alone.

The conditions are the substance of the job:

- **Shift work in the full sense** — nights, weekends, public holidays, and early starts
  that are genuinely early
- **Sleeping away from home** in freight and long-distance work
- **Solitary responsibility.** Modern operation is largely single-driver
- **Personenunfall.** Train drivers experience fatalities on the line. It is a known,
  statistically frequent occupational trauma, employers have structured after-care
  procedures, and anyone considering this work should know it in advance rather than discover
  it

## Pay {#pay}

**Market estimate.** Rail has several competing agreements — DB's with the **EVG** and the
**GDL**, plus separate agreements at private operators — so there is no single scale to
verify against, the same problem the repo records for
[bus drivers outside NRW](/jobs-in-germany/reference/pay/#reliability-of-these-figures).

| Stage | Gross/month |
|---|---|
| During Quereinstieg retraining | €2,200 – €2,800 |
| Entry, licensed | €2,900 – €3,400 |
| Experienced | €3,400 – €4,200 |
| Long-distance / specialised, senior | €4,000 – €4,800 |
| Instructor (Lehrlokführer), dispatcher, operations | €4,200 – €5,200 |

**The supplements are not a detail here.** Night, Sunday and holiday work is constant, most
of the supplement is **tax-free** under §3b EStG, and there are additional allowances for
overnight stays and unsocial rosters. Real take-home is meaningfully above the base — see
[shift work and supplements](/jobs-in-germany/reference/shift-work-and-supplements/).

Also relevant: rail collective bargaining has been unusually active, with disputes producing
both pay rises and **working-time reductions** — a phased move toward a 35-hour week for
shift staff, with an option to take the time or the money instead. Ask which model an
employer offers.

## Demand and outlook {#demand-and-outlook}

**Among the strongest in this repo, and demographically driven.** The workforce is old, a
large share retires this decade, and rail freight and passenger volumes are politically
committed to grow for climate reasons. Infrastructure investment is running at record levels.

Where the risk sits, honestly:

- **Automation.** Fully driverless mainline operation is not close in Germany — mixed
  traffic, legacy infrastructure and safety approval make it slow — but ETCS rollout and
  ATO-over-ETCS pilots are real. Metro systems are already driverless. Over a full career
  this is a live question, though not a near-term one.
- **The employer matters.** DB and the large private operators are tariff-bound; some
  smaller contractors are not.
- **Shift tolerance decides whether people stay.** Attrition in the first two years is
  substantial and it is almost entirely about the roster, not the driving.

## Foreign-trained candidates {#foreign-trained-candidates}

1. **The licence is the whole question.** No recognition of a job title is needed; without
   a TfV licence you may not drive.
2. **EU/EEA:** the Triebfahrzeugführerschein is an **EU licence and is recognised across
   member states** — one of the few genuinely portable credentials in this repo. You will
   still need a German employer's Zusatzbescheinigung, meaning German route and vehicle
   training.
3. **Third-country licences do not transfer.** Expect to qualify here from the beginning,
   via the Quereinstieg.
4. **Language: B2, and it is a hard requirement.** Radio communication with the dispatcher
   follows prescribed German wordings, and the operating rules are examined in German. EU
   rules set a floor of roughly B1 for the infrastructure's language; German operators ask
   for more, and the safety case is obvious.
5. **Visa:** difficult and worth being clear about. A **Quereinstieg retraining is not a
   qualification for §18a** — it is under the two-year minimum. The realistic routes are
   **§16a** to do the full three-year Ausbildung, **§26(2) BeschV** for Western Balkans
   nationals, or arriving on another basis and retraining once here.
6. **Medical and psychological fitness testing is conducted in German** and is a genuine
   filter.

## Pitfalls {#pitfalls}

- **The Zusatzbescheinigung does not move with you.** Factor the retraining time into any
  job change.
- **Read the tie-in clause** in an employer-funded retraining before signing.
- **The medical is career-defining.** Vision, hearing, cardiac and metabolic findings can end
  it; the psychological assessment screens for attention and stress response and is failed
  more often than people expect.
- **Freight and passenger are different lives.** Freight pays similarly, runs at night and
  keeps you away from home; regional passenger has predictable routes and more public
  contact.
- **Quereinstieg is not a shortcut to the Ausbildung's breadth.** If you may later want
  dispatch or operations roles, the three-year qualification is the stronger base.
