> Cuts metal to tolerance on CNC machines. The precision end of the industrial trades, and one where the gap between a qualified technician and a machine operator is wide, real, and routinely blurred in job ads.

## Fachrichtungen — certified, unlike its neighbour {#fachrichtungen--certified-unlike-its-neighbour}

| Fachrichtung | Focus |
|---|---|
| Drehmaschinensysteme | CNC turning |
| Fräsmaschinensysteme | CNC milling |
| Drehautomatensysteme | Automatic lathes, high-volume turned parts |
| Schleifmaschinensysteme | Grinding, the tightest tolerances |

Worth contrasting with [Industriemechaniker](/jobs-in-germany/industrial/industriemechaniker-in/), whose 2004 reform replaced certified specialisations with **Einsatzgebiete the employer defines**. Here the Fachrichtung **is on your certificate**, which makes the qualification more legible to employers and more restrictive at the same time. Two neighbouring trades, two opposite answers to the same design question.

## Fachkraft vs. Maschinenbediener — the distinction that costs money {#fachkraft-vs-maschinenbediener--the-distinction-that-costs-money}

A **Zerspanungsmechaniker/in** programmes, sets up (*rüsten*), tools, measures and troubleshoots. A **Maschinenbediener** loads parts, presses the cycle-start button and checks against a gauge — semi-skilled work, often agency-staffed, paid far less.

Both appear in job advertisements as "CNC operator", and employers will readily hire a skilled foreign machinist into the operator role because it is quick and needs no recognition. This is the same trap as [Rettungssanitäter vs Notfallsanitäter](/jobs-in-germany/healthcare/notfallsanitaeter-in/) and [Reinigungskraft vs Gebäudereiniger](/jobs-in-germany/hospitality/): the shorter role is real work, but it is not this profession, it does not pay like it, and it does not convert automatically. **Establish in writing which role you are being hired into.**

## What the job involves {#what-the-job-involves}

Reading drawings and deriving a machining strategy; selecting tools and cutting data; writing or editing programmes — shop-floor G-code and increasingly **CAM**; setting up and tooling machines; first-article inspection; and metrology to **micron** tolerances with gauges, micrometers and coordinate measuring machines. Quality documentation and SPC sit alongside.

Controls matter practically: **Siemens Sinumerik and Heidenhain dominate in Germany**, where Fanuc and Haas are more common elsewhere. Experience on the wrong control is a real, if short-lived, obstacle — say so plainly in applications rather than letting it surface later.

Sectors range from machine building and automotive to **aerospace and medical technology**, where traceability and documentation requirements are heavier and pay is better.

CNC machines run around the clock to amortise, so three-shift working is normal and supplements are a significant part of earnings.

## Qualification route {#qualification-route}

- **3.5 years dual**, industrial employer plus Berufsschule, **IHK** exam
- **Entry:** mittlerer Schulabschluss typical; formally open
- **Paid throughout:** roughly €1,150–1,400/month gross at IG Metall employers
- **Ladder:** **CNC-/CAM-Programmierer/in**, Einrichter, **Industriemeister Metall**, **staatlich geprüfte/r Techniker/in (Maschinenbau)**, or Qualitätsfachmann/QS roles

## Pay {#pay}

**IG Metall / IG BCE tariff, ERA-graded. Market estimates** — see the [category README](/jobs-in-germany/industrial/#pay-is-better-here-and-not-verifiable). Approximate gross monthly, 2026:

| Stage | Gross/month |
|---|---|
| Newly qualified (tariff-bound) | €3,300 – €3,900 |
| Experienced | €3,900 – €4,800 |
| CNC-/CAM-Programmierer, Einrichter | €4,300 – €5,200 |
| Industriemeister / Techniker | €4,700 – €5,800 |
| *(Maschinenbediener, for contrast)* | *€2,400 – €3,000, often via agency* |

Plus the 13th month, holiday pay, a 35-hour week at tariff employers, and three-shift supplements. The last row is there deliberately: it is the single most useful number in this file for anyone weighing a job offer.

## Demand and outlook {#demand-and-outlook}

Solid, with an ageing workforce and persistent difficulty filling skilled machining roles.

On automation, honestly: **this trade is genuinely automating** — automated loading, pallet systems and lights-out machining are widespread. What that displaces is the *operator* end. It increases the value of programming, setup, tooling strategy and metrology, which is exactly what the qualification covers. The qualified end of this trade sits on the right side of that split; the button-pressing end does not.

## Foreign candidates {#foreign-candidates}

1. **To work:** nothing required. Unregulated.
2. **For the visa:** **IHK FOSA** equivalence assessment.
3. **Highly portable.** CNC machining is taught and practised similarly worldwide, and the qualification travels well if you later leave Germany.
4. **Control familiarity** is the practical gap, not the machining — see above.
5. **Language: B1–B2.** Drawings, tolerance and quality documentation, safety instruction and shift handover are in German.

## Pitfalls {#pitfalls}

- **Being hired as a Maschinenbediener.** The largest and most common one. Check the contract title and the pay grade, not the advert.
- **Agency work (Leiharbeit)** is heavily used at the operator end of this sector.
- **Tariff vs. non-tariff** is worth 20–30% of the package; small job shops are often outside the agreement.
- **Fachrichtung is on the certificate** and narrows what some employers will consider.
- **Three-shift working** is the norm, not an exception to negotiate away.
