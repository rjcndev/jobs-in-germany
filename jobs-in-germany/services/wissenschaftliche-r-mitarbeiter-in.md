> German academia is built on fixed-term contracts, and a **specific statute exists to permit them**. The **WissZeitVG** disapplies the ordinary limits on repeated fixed terms for academic staff, which is why a research career here can mean a decade of two-year contracts, frequently part-time, with no permanent position to be promoted into. Anyone considering research in Germany should read this before committing.

## The WissZeitVG {#the-wisszeitvg}

Ordinary German employment law limits fixed-term contracts without an objective reason to **two years** — see [employment basics](/jobs-in-germany/reference/employment-basics/). The **Wissenschaftszeitvertragsgesetz** sets that aside for academic staff:

| Phase | Permitted fixed-term duration |
|---|---|
| **Qualification phase 1** — before the doctorate | **6 years** |
| **Qualification phase 2** — after the doctorate | **6 years** (**9** in medicine) |
| **Drittmittelbefristung** — externally funded posts | For the **duration of the funding**, largely outside the clock above |

So twelve years of lawful fixed-term employment without an objective reason, and grant-funded contracts alongside it. The clock is **personal and cumulative** — it follows you between universities and counts every qualifying contract you have held.

**The reform is a live political question.** Successive governments have proposed shortening the post-doctoral phase and requiring minimum contract lengths, under sustained public pressure from academic staff. The details have changed repeatedly and are still contested — **check the current text of the statute rather than any secondary account, including this one.**

## What that produces {#what-that-produces}

- **Contract lengths measured in one- and two-year steps**, frequently tied to a grant period rather than to a project's actual length
- **Part-time contracts for full-time work.** 50%, 65% and 75% posts are standard, particularly for doctoral researchers, and the workload is rarely scaled to match
- **No tenure track for most posts.** Unlike systems with assistant-professor ladders, the German route historically had one destination — a **professorship** — and nothing in between
- **The twelve-year cliff.** When the clock runs out and no professorship has been obtained, you are unemployable in the sector at anything other than a permanent post that mostly does not exist. People leave academia in their late thirties, involuntarily, having done everything asked of them

The partial remedy is the **Tenure-Track-Juniorprofessur**, introduced with federal funding from 2017: a junior professorship with a **defined route to permanency** on positive evaluation. It is a genuine improvement and there are not nearly enough of them.

## The roles, in order {#the-roles-in-order}

| Role | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Wissenschaftliche Hilfskraft (HiWi)** | Student assistant. Hourly, short contracts, minimal cover |
| **Wissenschaftliche/r Mitarbeiter/in (Promotion)** | Doctoral researcher, employed. Teaching duties are normal |
| **Stipendiat/in** | A doctorate funded by **scholarship** rather than employment — **no social insurance, no pension credit, no unemployment cover.** Tax-free, and much weaker than it looks |
| **Postdoc / Wiss. Mitarbeiter/in** | Post-doctoral research, still fixed-term |
| **Akademische/r Rat/Rätin** | The permanent-ish variant — sometimes **verbeamtet**; see [Beamte vs. Angestellte](/jobs-in-germany/reference/beamte-vs-angestellte/) |
| **Juniorprofessur (tenure track)** | W1, with evaluation toward permanency |
| **Professur** | **W2 / W3**, usually a civil-service appointment for life |
| **Lehrbeauftragte/r** | Adjunct teaching, paid per hour, **no social insurance**. Structurally precarious and widely used |

## Pay {#pay}

Academic staff are on **TV-L**, and the scale is the one verified for this repo, valid **01.04.2026 – 28.02.2027**.

| Role | Grade | Gross/month, **full-time** |
|---|---|---|
| Doctoral researcher | **E 13** | **€4,759 – €6,765** — but commonly at **50–75%**, so €2,400 – €5,100 actually paid |
| Postdoc | E 13 – E 14 | €4,759 – €7,300 |
| Akademische/r Rat/Rätin | A 13 – A 14, or E 13 – E 14 | Comparable; **net is higher if verbeamtet** |
| Juniorprofessur | **W 1** | Below E 13 full-time in several states — a known anomaly |
| Professur | **W 2 / W 3** | Base plus negotiated **Leistungsbezüge**, which vary enormously |

**The FTE fraction is the whole story at the doctoral stage.** E 13 is a good grade; 65% of it, for work that fills a week, is not. When comparing an offer, ask for the **percentage** first and the grade second.

Note also that **W-Besoldung is state law**, like all [Besoldung](/jobs-in-germany/reference/beamte-vs-angestellte/), so professorial pay differs by Bundesland — see [choosing a Bundesland](/jobs-in-germany/reference/bundeslaender/).

## Demand and outlook {#demand-and-outlook}

**Positions are plentiful; careers are not.** Germany funds a great deal of research — universities, the Max Planck, Fraunhofer, Helmholtz and Leibniz organisations, and a large third-party grant system — so there is no shortage of fixed-term posts.

What is scarce is permanence. The ratio of doctoral and post-doctoral positions to professorships is such that **most people in this system will not stay in it**, and the system is designed on that assumption.

Two things worth weighing:

- **The non-university research institutes** (Fraunhofer above all, and the applied Helmholtz centres) sit closer to industry, have more permanent positions and pay comparably. They are systematically underrated by people fixated on a university chair.
- **Industrial research** in Germany is large, well paid and actively recruits doctorates. Leaving academia here is a normal outcome, not a failure — the file [Ingenieur/in](/jobs-in-germany/engineering/ingenieur-in/) is where many of those careers land.

## Foreign candidates {#foreign-candidates}

1. **No recognition is required.** The occupation is unregulated; what matters is your degree and your record.
2. **Degree comparability** goes through **anabin/ZAB** for visa purposes — see [recognition authorities](/jobs-in-germany/reference/recognition-authorities/).
3. **Visa: §18d AufenthG is the route built for this** — the researcher permit, granted on a **hosting agreement** with a recognised research institution. It carries EU mobility rights for research stays in other member states and is usually simpler than the general skilled-worker route. **§18b** and the **Blue Card** are alternatives where the pay qualifies — which at full-time E 13 it generally does, and at 50% it does not.
4. **Language: it depends on the discipline more than on the job.** Much of the natural sciences, engineering and economics runs in English; the humanities, law and social sciences largely do not, and **teaching duties are usually in German**. Establish which before accepting.
5. **A scholarship is not employment.** If you are offered a Stipendium, understand that it provides no social insurance, no pension credit and no unemployment cover, and that your residence permit conditions may differ.
6. **The WissZeitVG clock counts contracts held in Germany**, including earlier ones. If you have worked here before, find out where you stand before planning a decade.

## Pitfalls {#pitfalls}

- **Ask for the FTE percentage and the contract length in the first conversation.** They matter more than the grade.
- **Know your position on the twelve-year clock**, in writing, from the personnel department.
- **Drittmittel contracts follow the money**, which can end before the work does.
- **A Stipendium is weaker than a contract** at the same headline figure.
- **Lehraufträge are not a career.** They are hourly teaching with no cover, and accumulating them does not lead anywhere.
- **Plan the exit from the start.** The people who do well out of German academia are the ones who keep an industry-viable profile throughout, not the ones who bet everything on the chair.
