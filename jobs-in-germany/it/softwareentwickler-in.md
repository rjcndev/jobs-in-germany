## Why this file is short on bureaucracy {#why-this-file-is-short-on-bureaucracy}

There is **no licence, no Anerkennung, and no protected title**. Anyone may call themselves a Softwareentwickler and be hired as one. Qualification recognition only matters *instrumentally*, for the visa — not for the right to do the work.

Note the one genuine exception: calling yourself **"Informatiker"** in the sense of a degree holder implies a degree, and "Ingenieur" is separately protected — see [Ingenieur/in](/jobs-in-germany/engineering/ingenieur-in/).

## Pay {#pay}

No collective agreement in most of the sector. Approximate **annual gross**, 2026 — the industry quotes yearly, not monthly, unlike the rest of this repo:

| Stage | Annual gross |
|---|---|
| Junior (0–2 yrs) | €50,000 – €62,000 |
| Mid (3–5 yrs) | €65,000 – €82,000 |
| Senior (6+ yrs) | €85,000 – €110,000 |
| Staff / Principal | €110,000 – €140,000+ |

Strong regional and sectoral spread. Munich, Frankfurt, Stuttgart and Berlin pay most; Munich has the cost of living to match. **Industry-tariff employers** — automotive and industrial firms under IG Metall agreements — pay very well at mid-level with 35-hour weeks and strong benefits, but compress at the top. US tech subsidiaries and funded startups pay the highest headline numbers; equity in German startups is frequently structured as VSOPs with poor realised outcomes, so discount it heavily.

German salaries look low next to US ones and are not directly comparable: no health insurance premium to self-fund, 28–30 days of leave, statutory sick pay, and strong dismissal protection after six months.

## Routes into the country {#routes-into-the-country}

1. **EU Blue Card (§18g AufenthG)** — the main route. Needs a recognised or comparable degree and a salary above an annually-set threshold; **IT and other shortage occupations get a substantially lower threshold**. The figures change every January — look up the current year's rather than trusting a number written down anywhere.
2. **IT specialist without a formal degree (§19c(2) AufenthG)** — a genuinely useful and underused route: professional experience in IT within the last years, a job offer above a salary floor, and — since the 2023 reform — **no German language requirement**. This is how self-taught and bootcamp-trained developers get in.
3. **Opportunity Card (Chancenkarte, §20a AufenthG)** — points-based, lets you enter to look for work.
4. **EU/EEA citizens** need none of this.

Your foreign degree should be checked against **anabin** (the ZAB database) for comparability — "entsprechend" or better is what the immigration office wants to see. This is an assessment of the degree, not a professional recognition procedure.

## Working culture notes {#working-culture-notes}

- **Arbeitszeugnis**: on leaving a job you receive a written reference in a coded formal register where bland phrasing carries grading. Have a German speaker read yours.
- **Probezeit**: six months probation, two weeks' notice either way. After that, dismissal protection is strong.
- **Works councils (Betriebsrat)** are common in larger firms and genuinely shape working conditions, including monitoring and on-call.
- **German is not required** at most product companies and startups in Berlin, Munich and Hamburg, and is often essential in Mittelstand, consultancies and public-sector work. It also determines whether you can participate in life outside the office.

## Pitfalls {#pitfalls}

- **Scheinselbständigkeit** — if you contract as a freelancer for essentially one client under their direction, authorities can reclassify you as an employee, with back social contributions owed by the client. This kills many long-term single-client contracts.
- **Blue Card thresholds change annually**; an offer that qualified in December may not in January.
- **Notice periods are long** by international standards — three months to quarter-end is common at senior level. It cuts both ways.
