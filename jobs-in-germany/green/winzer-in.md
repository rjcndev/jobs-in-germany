## Where the work is {#where-the-work-is}

German wine is regionally concentrated to an unusual degree, so **this is a profession that
decides where you live**:

**Rheinland-Pfalz** — Rheinhessen, Pfalz, Mosel, Nahe, Ahr — is the largest by far, followed
by **Baden** and **Württemberg**, then **Franken** in Bayern, with smaller regions in Saale-
Unstrut and Sachsen.

Note the examining-body quirk: **Rheinland-Pfalz has no Landwirtschaftskammer**. Its
**Dienstleistungszentren Ländlicher Raum (DLR)** do the job instead — which makes the
country's biggest wine region an immediate example of the
[sector's competent-authority variation](/jobs-in-germany/green/). Confirm the body before filing anything.

## Two jobs in one qualification {#two-jobs-in-one-qualification}


<div class="jig-table" markdown="1">

| **Weinberg** | **Keller** |
|---|---|
| Pruning, tying, canopy management, soil care | Pressing and must handling |
| Plant protection — with **Sachkunde** and a three-yearly refresher | Fermentation control, yeast, temperature |
| Harvest: timing, ripeness measurement, selection | Fining, filtration, blending, sulphur management |
| Vineyard establishment, rootstock and variety choice | Bottling, labelling, and **wine law** compliance |
| **Steillagen** — steep-slope work, by hand or monorack | Laboratory analysis and sensory assessment |

</div>


And increasingly a third: **selling**. Estate wine is sold direct — cellar door, wine
festivals, mailing lists, online, and to restaurants — so marketing, hospitality and language
are a large part of a small estate's survival. This is the part that a technically excellent
winemaker most often lacks.

**Steillagen deserve their own note.** The steep Mosel and Mittelrhein slopes cannot be
mechanised, cost several times more per hectare to work than flat sites, and are being
abandoned where the economics fail. They also produce the wines the region is known for. It
is the sharpest tension in German viticulture.

## The economics {#the-economics}


<div class="jig-table" markdown="1">

| Structure | How it works |
|---|---|
| **Family estate (Weingut)** | Owns or leases vineyards, makes and sells its own wine. Succession is the normal route in |
| **Winzergenossenschaft** | A cooperative: members grow, the co-op vinifies and sells. **A significant employer**, and the route into cellar work without owning land |
| **Fassweinbetrieb** | Grows and sells wine in bulk to bottlers. Price-taking and squeezed |
| **Employed at a large estate or a Kellerei** | Salaried work, including the **Kellermeister** role |

</div>


Vineyard land in established regions is expensive, which makes buying in hard and makes
**succession the dominant route** — as in pharmacy, and with the same consequence: the
qualification is widely held by people who will run a business, and employed career paths are
thinner than the qualification's prestige suggests.

## Qualification and what sits above {#qualification-and-what-sits-above}

- **3 years dual**, estate plus Berufsschule
- **Above it:** **Winzermeister/in**; **Techniker Weinbau und Oenologie**; or the degree route
  — **Weinbau und Oenologie** at Geisenheim and elsewhere, which leads to Kellermeister and
  technical-director roles, and to the wine trade, analysis and consultancy
- **Pflanzenschutz-Sachkunde** is effectively required

## Demand and outlook {#demand-and-outlook}

**Honest, and mixed.**

- **Climate change is the defining force.** Harvests have moved weeks earlier, alcohol levels
  have risen, and classic German cool-climate styles are harder to make. Regions once
  marginal now ripen reliably; drought and extreme events are the new risk. **PIWI**
  fungus-resistant varieties are expanding because they need far less plant protection.
- **German wine consumption is falling**, in line with a broad decline in alcohol
  consumption. This is a shrinking domestic market, and the file would be dishonest not to
  say so.
- **The top end is doing well**; the bulk-wine end is under severe price pressure.
- **Succession failures** are widespread, and vineyard area is slowly contracting.
- **Qualified cellar and vineyard staff are nonetheless scarce**, because few people train
  for a sector with this profile.

Growth where it exists: direct sales and wine tourism, organic and PIWI production, sparkling
wine, and export.

## Foreign candidates {#foreign-candidates}

1. **To work: no recognition required.**
2. **For the visa: yes** — **Landwirtschaftskammer**, or the **DLR** in Rheinland-Pfalz.
3. **Language: B1–B2 for the work, C1 to sell.** Small estates hire people who can talk to
   customers, and that is where the value is.
4. **Pflanzenschutz-Sachkunde is German.**
5. **Visa:** §18a, **§16a**, or **§26(2) BeschV**. Harvest work is the seasonal route and
   leads nowhere on its own.
6. **Foreign winemaking experience transfers well technically** — the international harvest
   circuit is a well-used way in — but German wine law, labelling and the Prädikat system are
   specific and are where assessments find gaps.

## Pitfalls {#pitfalls}

- **This profession chooses your Bundesland.** Decide the region first.
- **Employment and ownership are different careers.** If you will not inherit or buy, aim at
  cooperatives, large estates and Kellermeister roles deliberately.
- **Steillagen are romantic and economically brutal.** Understand what you are taking on.
- **Selling is half the job** at a small estate, and it is the half most Ausbildungen
  underweight.
- **Wine law is unforgiving** — labelling, yields, designations and documentation are
  regulated and inspected.
