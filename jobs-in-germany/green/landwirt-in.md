## What the job involves {#what-the-job-involves}

- **Ackerbau** — soil, sowing, crop protection, harvest, and the agronomy behind the decisions
- **Tierhaltung** — dairy, pigs, poultry, beef; feeding, health, breeding, milking systems
- **Technik** — operating and maintaining machinery that now costs as much as a house, with GPS guidance, section control and yield mapping as normal equipment
- **Dokumentation** — and this is a larger part of the job than outsiders expect: fertiliser records under the **Düngeverordnung**, plant-protection records, animal movement and medicine records, and the conditionality requirements attached to EU support payments
- **Betriebsführung** — on a family farm, the business is the job: subsidies, markets, contracts, investment

Farms are increasingly specialised, and a Landwirt in a 600-cow dairy and one on an arable unit are doing different work with the same qualification.

## The structural split: east and west {#the-structural-split-east-and-west}

This shapes what "a job in agriculture" means more than anything else:

| | **Western Germany** | **Eastern Germany** |
|---|---|---|
| Typical unit | **Family farm**, often under 100 ha | **Large enterprise**, frequently several hundred to several thousand hectares — successors to the LPG cooperatives |
| Employment | Family labour; few salaried posts | **Salaried employment is normal**, with shift structures and specialised roles |
| Route in | Succession, marriage, or buying in — all very hard | **Applying for a job**, like any other sector |

**For anyone without a farm in the family, the eastern structure is the accessible one**, and it is systematically overlooked. It also offers something family farms usually cannot: defined hours, a wage, and a specialism.

## Qualification route {#qualification-route}

- **3 years dual**, farm plus Berufsschule; examined by the **Landwirtschaftskammer** or a state authority — **not** the IHK or HWK. See the [sector README](/jobs-in-germany/green/), and confirm which body is competent in your Bundesland before applying for anything
- **Paid throughout**, at modest rates
- **Above it:** **Landwirtschaftsmeister** — the classic route, and the qualification to run a farm and train apprentices; **Agrartechniker**; **Agrarbetriebswirt**; or a Bachelor in Agrarwissenschaften, which opens advisory, breeding, machinery and agribusiness careers
- **Pflanzenschutz-Sachkunde** is effectively required, and carries a **refresher obligation every three years** — see [occupational certificates](/jobs-in-germany/reference/occupational-certificates/)

## Pay {#pay}

**Market estimate.** Agricultural pay follows regional agreements negotiated by IG BAU, with patchy coverage; many farms are not bound.

| Stage | Gross/month |
|---|---|
| Qualified Landwirt/in, employed | €2,600 – €3,200 |
| Experienced, with a specialism (dairy herd, machinery) | €3,100 – €3,800 |
| **Herdenmanager/in**, large livestock unit | €3,600 – €4,500 |
| **Landwirtschaftsmeister / Betriebsleiter** | €3,900 – €5,200 |
| Farm owner | Not comparable — income is a business result, and in poor years it is negative |

The statutory **Mindestlohn** is the floor and is a perishable figure, not quoted here.

Two things the table cannot show. **Housing is frequently part of the package**, valued at statutory rates, which changes the real figure in both directions. And **hours are long and seasonal** — harvest and calving do not respect the Arbeitszeitgesetz's intentions, though the Act still applies.

## Saisonarbeit and the 70-day rule {#saisonarbeit-and-the-70-day-rule}

German fruit, vegetable, asparagus and wine harvests run on **kurzfristige Beschäftigung** under **§8 SGB IV**: employment limited to **three months or 70 working days** in a calendar year, which is **free of social-insurance contributions** and must not be the worker's livelihood (*berufsmäßig*). See [Minijob and geringfügige Beschäftigung](/jobs-in-germany/reference/minijob-und-geringfuegige-beschaeftigung/) for how it sits alongside the Minijob.

Several hundred thousand people do this work each year, overwhelmingly from **Romania and Poland**. It is a structural feature of German agriculture: the asparagus harvest in particular could not happen without it.

**What is genuinely true of the arrangement:**

- **The Mindestlohn applies in full.** Seasonal work is not exempt from it
- **Accommodation and meals may be deducted**, but only up to the statutory Sachbezugswerte — not at whatever the employer charges
- **Since 2022 the employer must ensure seasonal workers have health-insurance cover**, and evidence it. This closed the worst gap in the model: before it, workers were commonly uninsured for the whole period
- **Accident insurance applies from the first hour** through the agricultural Berufsgenossenschaft, whatever the contract says
- **Working time must be recorded**

**And what the record shows going wrong**, repeatedly and in documented investigations: piece rates set so that the minimum wage cannot be reached in the hours recorded; accommodation charged above the permitted rates, or for beds in overcrowded rooms; hours worked and not recorded; deductions for tools and transport; and contracts in a language the worker does not read. Enforcement is by the **Finanzkontrolle Schwarzarbeit** — the customs unit staffed by the [Zollbeamte](/jobs-in-germany/public-service/zollbeamte-r/) profiled in this repo — and by the agricultural inspectorates, and both find these patterns when they look.

**If you are considering seasonal work here:** get the contract in writing before travelling, check what is deducted for accommodation and food, confirm health-insurance cover in writing, record your own hours daily, and know that the minimum wage is not negotiable whatever the piece rate implies. Advisory services for seasonal workers exist in several languages and are free.

This is not the same thing as the qualified occupation the rest of this file describes, and the two should not be confused — which is precisely why they are both here.

## Demand and outlook {#demand-and-outlook}

**Qualified people are scarce; the sector is under pressure.**

- **Shortage of trained Landwirte and especially of herd managers and machinery specialists** is real and long-standing. Farms compete for them.
- **Farm numbers keep falling** while average size rises. Succession is the sector's biggest single problem: a large share of farms have no successor.
- **Regulation is intensifying** — fertiliser rules, plant-protection restrictions, animal welfare standards and emissions requirements — and it is the source of most of the sector's political conflict. Whatever your view of it, the direction is not reversing, and compliance competence is increasingly what a farm employer is buying.
- **Growth areas:** biogas and on-farm energy, precision agriculture and data, organic production, and direct marketing.

## Foreign candidates {#foreign-candidates}

1. **To work: no recognition required.** The title is not protected.
2. **For the visa: yes**, and the competent body is the **Landwirtschaftskammer** or a state authority — **not IHK FOSA and not the Handwerkskammer**. Identify it for your target Bundesland first; this is the most common wasted application in the green sector.
3. **Pflanzenschutz-Sachkunde is German** and does not transfer.
4. **Language: B1–B2.** Machinery safety, animal health, and the documentation.
5. **Visa:** §18a with recognition, **§16a** to train here, or **§26(2) BeschV**.
6. **Seasonal work is a different legal world.** EU citizens simply come. Third-country nationals need a specific seasonal-employment permission, it is limited, and it does not lead to a skilled-worker permit.

## Pitfalls {#pitfalls}

- **Applying to the wrong chamber** costs months.
- **"Room and board included" needs a number.** Ask what is deducted and check it against the Sachbezugswerte.
- **Family-farm employment blurs work and life.** Get hours, holiday and accommodation in a written contract even when the arrangement is friendly.
- **The east is where the jobs are** if you do not have a farm to inherit.
- **Machinery is the highest-risk part of the work.** German agriculture's accident and fatality rate is high, PTO and overturning accidents dominate, and the agricultural Berufsgenossenschaft's rules are the ones to know.
